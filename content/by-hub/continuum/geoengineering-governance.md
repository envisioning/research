# Geoengineering Governance Regimes

## Summary

Global oversight for solar radiation and carbon removal projects.

## Description

The prospect of deliberately manipulating Earth's climate system through large-scale technological interventions presents unprecedented governance challenges that existing international frameworks were never designed to address. Geoengineering Governance Regimes represent emerging multilateral structures aimed at establishing clear decision-making processes, liability frameworks, and monitoring requirements for climate intervention technologies, particularly solar radiation management techniques that could reflect sunlight away from Earth and carbon dioxide removal methods that extract greenhouse gases from the atmosphere. These governance frameworks operate through a combination of treaty mechanisms, international protocols, and coordinating bodies that define who has the authority to approve such interventions, under what circumstances they may be deployed, and how their effects will be monitored across borders. The technical architecture of these regimes typically includes provisions for transparent research protocols, mandatory impact assessments that evaluate regional climate effects, liability mechanisms for unintended consequences, and critically, procedures for managing "termination shock"—the rapid warming that could occur if solar radiation management were suddenly halted. By establishing these structures before widespread deployment, governance regimes aim to prevent scenarios where individual nations or private entities could alter global climate patterns without international consensus.

The fundamental challenge these frameworks address is the inherently transboundary nature of geoengineering interventions, where actions taken in one region inevitably affect climate patterns, precipitation, and agricultural productivity across the entire planet. Without coordinated governance, the world faces scenarios where unilateral deployment by a single nation experiencing severe climate impacts could trigger international conflicts, as the same intervention that reduces temperatures in one hemisphere might disrupt monsoon patterns critical to food security in another. These regimes also tackle the thorny question of consent—determining what threshold of international agreement is necessary before planet-scale interventions can proceed, and how to balance the interests of nations facing existential climate threats against those potentially harmed by intervention side effects. Furthermore, they establish monitoring and verification systems to ensure compliance with agreed-upon deployment limits and research protocols, preventing the gradual escalation of interventions without proper oversight. By creating clear liability frameworks, these governance structures also address who bears responsibility when geoengineering projects cause environmental damage or economic losses, providing mechanisms for compensation and dispute resolution that could prevent interventions from becoming sources of geopolitical tension.

While comprehensive global geoengineering governance remains largely aspirational, foundational elements are emerging through existing international bodies and new initiatives. The United Nations Environment Programme and various scientific advisory groups have begun developing preliminary frameworks for research governance, establishing principles that emphasize transparency, inclusivity, and precaution in early-stage geoengineering studies. Some regional bodies have implemented moratoria on certain types of climate intervention pending the development of adequate oversight mechanisms, while academic institutions and research consortia have adopted voluntary codes of conduct for outdoor experiments. The trajectory of these governance efforts will likely accelerate as climate impacts intensify and pressure builds to consider more aggressive interventions. Looking forward, effective geoengineering governance will need to balance the urgency of climate action against the profound risks of poorly coordinated planetary-scale interventions, ensuring that if humanity does choose to deliberately engineer Earth's climate, such decisions emerge from inclusive, transparent processes that protect the interests of all nations and future generations. These frameworks represent not just technical regulatory structures but fundamental questions about global cooperation, intergenerational responsibility, and humanity's relationship with planetary systems.
