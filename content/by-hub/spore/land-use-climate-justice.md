# Land Use & Climate Justice

## Summary

Allocation of land between biomass production, carbon farming, and restoration.

## Description

The intersection of land use and climate justice represents a critical framework for addressing one of agriculture's most pressing ethical and practical challenges: how to fairly allocate finite land resources among competing demands for food production, carbon sequestration, and ecological restoration. This approach recognizes that land is not merely a productive asset but a contested resource with profound implications for climate mitigation, biodiversity conservation, and social equity. The core mechanism involves developing decision-making frameworks that weigh multiple objectives—from feeding growing populations to meeting carbon reduction targets—while ensuring that the benefits and burdens of land allocation decisions are distributed equitably across communities, particularly those most vulnerable to climate change. This requires integrating spatial analysis, stakeholder engagement processes, and policy tools that can balance agricultural productivity with carbon farming initiatives like afforestation or soil carbon sequestration, alongside the restoration of degraded ecosystems.

The agricultural sector faces mounting pressure to simultaneously increase food production, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and reverse biodiversity loss, all while working with a fixed and often degrading land base. Traditional approaches to land allocation have frequently prioritized short-term economic returns or single objectives, leading to outcomes where industrial agriculture expands at the expense of carbon-rich forests or where conservation initiatives displace smallholder farmers without adequate compensation. Land use and climate justice frameworks address these problems by making visible the trade-offs inherent in different allocation scenarios and ensuring that decision-making processes include the voices of Indigenous communities, small-scale farmers, and other groups whose livelihoods and cultural practices depend on land access. This approach enables the development of more nuanced strategies that might, for example, prioritize agroforestry systems that provide both food security and carbon sequestration, or establish compensation mechanisms for communities that steward land for ecosystem services rather than commodity production.

Early applications of land use and climate justice principles are emerging in regional planning initiatives and national climate strategies, particularly in countries developing their contributions to international climate agreements. Some jurisdictions are experimenting with participatory mapping processes that allow local communities to identify priority areas for different land uses based on their knowledge and needs, while others are implementing payment schemes that compensate landholders for carbon storage or biodiversity conservation. These efforts connect to broader movements toward regenerative agriculture and nature-based climate solutions, which recognize that effective climate action in the land sector requires not just technical interventions but fundamental shifts in how societies value and govern land resources. As climate targets become more ambitious and the competition for land intensifies, frameworks that explicitly address justice dimensions will become increasingly essential for ensuring that the transition to sustainable land management does not reproduce or exacerbate existing inequalities, but instead creates pathways toward more equitable and resilient food and ecological systems.
