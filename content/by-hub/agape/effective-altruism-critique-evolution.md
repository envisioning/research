# Effective Altruism Critique & Evolution

## Summary

Post-FTX reckoning with effective altruism, challenging its assumptions while elements are absorbed into mainstream philanthropy.

## Description

The collapse of FTX and the subsequent legal proceedings against Sam Bankman-Fried have catalyzed an unprecedented examination of effective altruism (EA), a philosophical and social movement that emerged in the early 2010s advocating for the use of evidence and reason to determine the most effective ways to benefit others. At its core, EA rests on several key principles: the moral imperative to do the most good possible with available resources, the use of quantitative analysis to compare different interventions, and a willingness to consider unconventional or neglected causes. The movement gained significant traction in philanthropic circles through organizations promoting rigorous cost-effectiveness analysis, such as evaluating charitable interventions in terms of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) or similar metrics. However, the FTX scandal—in which billions of dollars ostensibly earmarked for effective giving were lost through what prosecutors alleged was fraud—has exposed fundamental tensions within the movement. Critics have highlighted EA's demographic concentration among young, predominantly white, male technologists and philosophers, its sometimes uncomfortable proximity to Silicon Valley wealth accumulation, and its evolution toward "longtermism," a philosophical stance prioritizing the welfare of potential future generations over present needs, which some argue can justify morally questionable means in pursuit of speculative ends.

The critique of effective altruism addresses several interconnected challenges that resonate beyond the movement itself to broader questions of philanthropic practice. First, the emphasis on quantifiable impact has been challenged for potentially overlooking systemic issues, power dynamics, and the voices of beneficiary communities themselves. Research in development economics and social justice philanthropy suggests that the most easily measured interventions are not always the most transformative, and that metrics-driven approaches can inadvertently reinforce existing power structures by privileging the perspectives of donors and analysts over those of affected communities. Second, EA's relationship with wealth generation—particularly the "earn to give" philosophy that encouraged individuals to pursue lucrative careers to maximize their charitable contributions—has faced scrutiny for potentially creating moral hazard and rationalizing ethically questionable business practices. Third, the movement's intellectual insularity and its tendency to defer to a relatively small group of philosophers and researchers on questions of cause prioritization has raised concerns about democratic accountability in philanthropy. These critiques have prompted soul-searching within the EA community and sparked broader conversations about the role of measurement, expertise, and beneficiary participation in philanthropic decision-making.

Despite the reputational damage from recent scandals, core elements of the effective altruism framework continue to permeate mainstream philanthropic practice, suggesting a complex legacy rather than wholesale rejection. Major foundations and individual donors increasingly incorporate rigorous impact evaluation, comparative cost-effectiveness analysis, and systematic cause prioritization into their grantmaking processes, even as they distance themselves from the EA label. The movement's emphasis on neglected causes and its willingness to fund unglamorous but high-impact interventions has influenced how philanthropic institutions think about portfolio diversification and risk-taking. However, this absorption is occurring alongside significant evolution: many practitioners are integrating EA's analytical tools with approaches that emphasize community leadership, participatory grantmaking, and attention to systemic change and power dynamics. Some organizations are developing hybrid models that combine quantitative impact analysis with qualitative assessments of beneficiary agency and structural transformation. Looking forward, the effective altruism critique represents a pivotal moment in philanthropy's ongoing struggle to balance rigor with humility, efficiency with equity, and the desire to do measurable good with recognition of the limits of measurement itself. The debate ultimately reflects fundamental questions about whose knowledge counts in determining social priorities, how philanthropy should relate to wealth and power, and whether doing good can be reduced to calculation or requires ongoing dialogue, accountability, and moral imagination.
