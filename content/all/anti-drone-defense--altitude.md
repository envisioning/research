# Counter-UAS (C-UAS) for Airport Protection

## Summary

Technologies to detect and mitigate unauthorized drone activity near airports.

## Description

The rapid proliferation of consumer and commercial drones has introduced a critical vulnerability to airport operations, where even a small unmanned aircraft system (UAS) can trigger runway closures, flight diversions, and significant economic disruption. Counter-UAS systems represent a multi-layered technological approach to detecting, tracking, identifying, and neutralizing unauthorized drone activity within controlled airspace. These systems typically integrate several complementary detection methods: radar arrays tuned to identify small, slow-moving objects; radio frequency (RF) sensors that intercept communication signals between drones and their operators; electro-optical and infrared cameras for visual confirmation; and acoustic sensors that can detect characteristic rotor signatures. Once a threat is identified, C-UAS platforms employ various mitigation techniques ranging from RF jamming that disrupts command-and-control links, to GPS spoofing that redirects the drone to a safe landing zone, to physical capture methods using net-launching devices or trained birds of prey in some deployments.

The aviation industry faces a delicate balance between maintaining security and preserving operational efficiency in an increasingly congested electromagnetic environment. Traditional airport security measures were never designed to address threats arriving from above, and conventional air traffic control systems struggle to detect objects smaller than light aircraft. Counter-UAS technology addresses this gap by providing dedicated surveillance of the low-altitude airspace surrounding airport perimeters, typically extending several kilometres beyond runway thresholds where drone encounters pose the greatest collision risk. However, implementation challenges are substantial: RF jamming techniques must avoid interfering with legitimate aviation communications, navigation aids, and cellular networks; detection systems must distinguish between actual threats and birds, weather phenomena, or authorized drone operations; and legal frameworks governing the authority to disable or destroy private property remain ambiguous in many jurisdictions. The technology also raises privacy concerns, as RF detection capabilities can inadvertently intercept communications beyond airport boundaries.

Major international airports have begun deploying C-UAS systems following high-profile incidents, with early adopters reporting improved situational awareness and faster response times to drone incursions. Industry analysts note that effective airport protection increasingly requires integration with broader air traffic management systems and coordination with local law enforcement agencies who maintain legal authority over airspace enforcement. Research suggests that future C-UAS architectures will incorporate artificial intelligence for automated threat assessment, reducing false alarms while accelerating decision-making during genuine incidents. As regulatory bodies worldwide develop frameworks for urban air mobility and expanded drone operations, the evolution of Counter-UAS technology will prove essential to maintaining the safety and efficiency of commercial aviation while enabling the legitimate growth of the drone industry in shared airspace.
